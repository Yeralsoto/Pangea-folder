There is no American rental market. There are about a hundred and sixty-five.

Ninety-two of them are posting negative rent growth while the Twin Cities run at +4.6%. The national figure describes neither.

September 2026 · 9 min read

National rent fell about 1.5% year over year through February 2026. That single number is the least useful fact in this piece, because 92 of the 165 major metros are negative and the rest are not, and the gap between the two ends is nearly ten points.

Read the national figure and you will underwrite the wrong half of the country.

This is a supply story, not a demand story

The instinct is to read the losing column as places people are leaving. They are not. Austin, Denver, Phoenix and Tampa still take population. What they also took was an enormous delivery pipeline, and the pipeline landed into a market that had already priced the growth.

Concessions did the rest. A quoted rent with two months free is not that rent, and it does not renew at that rent either.

The Midwest did not win. It simply never got a supply wave, so nothing had to be given back.

The winning column is mostly markets with limited new deliveries and an affordability advantage that never went away. The Northeast is projected to run 4–5% annually and the Midwest 3–4.5%. Neither is exciting. Both are bankable.

Land tells the same story a year earlier

Cross the rent table against finished-lot supply and the pattern repeats. Austin, Atlanta and Denver read as significantly oversupplied on lots. Los Angeles and Philadelphia still do not.

That is the practical value of watching land even if you never buy any: it is the earliest honest signal in the chain.

What this changes about a deal

  • Underwrite the submarket, never the metro. Nashville and Austin both contain streets that are tightening. The metro average will not find them.
  • Ask what has been delivered, not what is planned. Planned units get cancelled. Delivered units compete with you on the day they open.
  • Treat a falling metro as an entry, not a veto. San Antonio at −5.1% is a bad place to own a lease-up finished last year and a reasonable place to buy one from someone who did.
  • Check concessions in the comps. In the losing column they are doing a lot of quiet work on the quoted numbers.
  • In the winning column, check why. Limited deliveries can mean a constrained market or a market nobody wants to build in. Those are different.
