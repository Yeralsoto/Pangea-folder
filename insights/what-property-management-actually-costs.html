The eight per cent is not the cost of property management.

A worked example of where an operating year actually leaks, and why the management fee is almost never the number to argue about.

September 2026 · 6 min read

A worked example, not a client file. The figures are illustrative and chosen because they are ordinary.

Owners negotiate the management fee harder than anything else in the operating budget. It is visible, it is a percentage, and it feels like the one line you control.

Take a small building: eight units, $1,800 a month each, $172,800 gross potential. Management at 8% is $13,824. Argue it down to 6% and you have saved $3,456.

Now look at what else happened that year.

Where the year actually went

  • Vacancy and turnover. Two units turned. Three weeks empty each, plus paint, clean and a lock change. Call it $4,600 in lost rent and $2,400 in make-ready.
  • Delinquency. One tenant went two months down before anyone moved. $3,600, most of which never comes back.
  • Deferred maintenance. A roof repair postponed twice became a roof repair plus a ceiling. The difference is not the roof, it is the ceiling.
  • Under-market rent. Three units never got the renewal increase because nobody ran the comps in time. At $75 a month each, that is $2,700 this year and it compounds into the next.

You saved $3,456 on the fee. Turnover alone cost twice that, and the rent you did not raise costs it again every year.

$3,456Saved by arguing the fee down two points
$13,300Leaked elsewhere in the same year
3.8×The second number over the first

What the fee is actually buying

The manager who charges 8% and holds turnover to one unit, catches the delinquency in week two and brings you the renewal comps unprompted is not more expensive than the one at 6%. They are several thousand dollars cheaper, and the gap widens every year you hold.

This is also why operations belong in the underwriting. A rental model with a management fee line and no turnover assumption, no delinquency allowance and no annual rent review is not modelling the building. It is modelling a spreadsheet.

What we would actually check

  • Turnover rate and average days vacant, in writing, for the manager's existing portfolio.
  • How delinquency is escalated, and on what day.
  • Whether anyone is contractually responsible for reviewing rents at renewal.
  • The maintenance reserve, and whether last year's deferred items are in this year's budget.
  • What the manager does not do, which is usually where the surprise lives.
